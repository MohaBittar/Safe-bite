    💊 G6PD Deficiency

    G6PD Deficiency

    An inherited condition that affects red blood cells. Certain foods, medications, and infections can trigger hemolytic anemia.

    Key triggers: Fava beans (broad beans), certain legumes, moth beans, soy products (in some cases), and certain medications.

    ⚠️ Severe Allergic Reaction (Anaphylaxis) Symptoms

    Respiratory

    • Difficulty breathing or swallowing
    • Wheezing or shortness of breath
    • Swelling of tongue or throat
    • Hoarse voice

    Skin

    • Widespread hives or rash
    • Swelling of face, lips, or eyes
    • Flushed or pale skin
    • Itching

    Circulatory

    • Dizziness or fainting
    • Drop in blood pressure
    • Rapid or weak pulse
    • Loss of consciousness

    Other

    • Nausea, vomiting, or diarrhea
    • Feeling of doom
    • Confusion or anxiety
    • Abdominal pain or cramps

    💊 G6PD Hemolytic Crisis Symptoms

    Physical Signs

    • Dark or tea-colored urine
    • Yellow skin or eyes (jaundice)
    • Pale skin
    • Enlarged spleen (pain in upper left abdomen)

    General Symptoms

    • Extreme fatigue or weakness
    • Dizziness or lightheadedness
    • Shortness of breath
    • Rapid heart rate

    Severe Signs

    • Back or abdominal pain
    • Fever
    • Fainting
    • Dark brown or black urine (severe hemolysis)

    📃 Emergency Action Steps

    1

    Call Emergency Services

    Call 911 (or your local emergency number) immediately. Do not wait.

    2

    Use Epinephrine (if available)

    If you have an epinephrine auto-injector (EpiPen), use it on the outer thigh. It can be repeated after 5-15 minutes if symptoms persist.

    3

    Position the Person

    For allergic reactions: Lay the person down with legs elevated. For breathing difficulty: Help them sit upright. For vomiting: Place them on their side.

    4

    Stay Calm & Monitor

    Stay with the person. Monitor breathing and consciousness. Note what was eaten and when. Bring the food packaging to the hospital if possible.

    5

    Go to the Hospital

    Even if symptoms improve, always go to the emergency room. Biphasic reactions can occur hours later.
